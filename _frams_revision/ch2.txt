[114] CHAPTER II

[115] REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES

[117] This chapter reviews literature and studies relevant to the Financial Risk Assessment and Micro-Lending System (FRAMS). It discusses local and foreign work on credit-risk management, microfinance, digital lending, repayment monitoring, and information-system use. The review identifies practices that inform the system’s design and distinguishes those practices from features that FRAMS does not implement. FRAMS uses configured rules to produce an eligibility estimate; it does not use machine learning to predict default or make the final lending decision.

[118] Related Literature

[119] Local Literature

[120] Mina (2024) examined credit-risk management practices in microfinance lending institutions in Nueva Ecija. The descriptive study involved four lending officers from San Isidro and Cabiao. The institutions offered individual and group loans, often collected weekly, and used interviews and applicant checks to review creditworthiness. Checks included reviewing the applicant’s credit situation, confirming that the business existed, and determining whether the borrower had an outstanding loan with another institution. Mina also identified weaknesses in existing practices and proposed measures to manage remaining risks. This study is relevant to FRAMS because it highlights the importance of consistently recording borrower information and applying documented checks. FRAMS supports those tasks through borrower records, supporting documents, and a rule-based eligibility estimate; it does not independently verify external debts or business existence unless staff review the submitted information.

[122] Juventud et al. (2023) discussed credit risk in microfinance lending to micro-enterprises. They described microfinance as a source of financial support for low-income households, while noting that limited credit data, limited credit histories, and over-indebtedness can increase lending risk. The authors also discussed group lending and selected forms of collateral as risk-management practices. These points establish why lenders need relevant borrower information and a consistent assessment process. FRAMS uses the income, obligations, loan amount, term, and other configured criteria available in its records; it does not rely on a credit-bureau score.

[124] Estrellado (2024) studied financial-inclusion practices of lending institutions and the sustainability of micro-businesses in Sorsogon City. The descriptive study involved 15 micro-business owners and considered factors such as years in operation, initial capital, loan amount, and daily sales. The reported strategies included reducing loan-access barriers, using loans as business capital, offering repayment options, and providing financial-literacy education. Estrellado recommended improved credit-information sharing, borrower monitoring, need-based financial education, and access to credit. The study broadens the context of FRAMS by showing that loan management concerns extend beyond assessment to repayment and borrower support. FRAMS includes application processing and repayment records, but it does not provide a separate financial-literacy module.

[126] In a related local study, Juventud et al. (2023) assessed perceived credit risk among micro-enterprise owners in Barangay Población, Iligan City. The descriptive-correlational study included 75 business owners. The authors described limited income and credit history as common concerns and discussed practices such as group lending, collateral, and cash-flow assessment. They also reported that age, business type, previous loan use, and loan experience did not significantly explain respondents’ perceptions of credit risk. For FRAMS, the study supports collecting financial information relevant to repayment capacity, while its findings do not establish that the system’s particular eligibility rules are predictive or validated for other populations.

[127] Foreign Literature

[128] Kou et al. (2022) examined machine-learning approaches to financial-risk assessment on digital lending platforms. Their work discussed the use of real-time data and adaptive risk algorithms to update assessments as borrower behavior changes. This literature provides context for automated risk assessment, but its methods should not be conflated with the approach used in FRAMS. FRAMS does not implement a machine-learning score; it applies configured eligibility rules and presents the result for review by the authorized administrator.

[130] Bazarbash and Beaton (2020) examined fintech micro-lending in emerging economies and discussed how automated scoring and alternative data, including transaction-related information, may support financial inclusion while managing risk. The relevant implication for FRAMS is the value of a structured and consistently applied assessment process. FRAMS does not collect mobile-use data or implement the alternative-data scoring methods described in that work.

[132] Ghosh et al. (2023) discussed predictive analytics for identifying potential delinquency in microfinance. Chen and Li (2023) examined artificial-intelligence approaches that reassess risk during the life of a loan, while Frost et al. (2024) discussed digital lending platforms using agile development and real-time risk analytics. Rahman and Hasan (2025) emphasized modular system design and ongoing monitoring of changing borrower profiles. These sources describe capabilities associated with predictive or adaptive systems. FRAMS has a narrower scope: it records installment status, payment history, overdue penalties, and reports, but it does not use a predictive model to identify likely default or automatically revise repayment terms.

[134] Taken together, the foreign literature describes approaches that may inform future development but are outside the current FRAMS implementation. The present study focuses on a locally hosted web application that keeps borrower information, applications, eligibility estimates, loan schedules, payments, and collection records within one system. This distinction is important when interpreting the project’s functionality and results.

[135] Related Studies

[136] Local Studies

[137] Santos (2023) discussed changes in the Philippine financial landscape associated with digital payments and mobile banking and highlighted the relevance of online access to financial services. This work provides context for offering a borrower-facing portal and digital access to loan information in FRAMS. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that FRAMS improves trust or financial outcomes; those effects would require direct evaluation.

[138] The Iligan City study by Juventud et al. (2023) is also relevant as an empirical local study because it examines perceived credit risk among micro-enterprise owners. Its discussion of limited credit history supports the practical value of recording declared income and existing obligations when a formal credit-bureau score is unavailable. However, the study’s findings should be treated as contextual support rather than validation of FRAMS’s eligibility formula.

[139] Ramirez (2024) developed and validated a tool for assessing risk and return in the gross loan portfolio of microfinance institutions in Occidental Mindoro, illustrating the role of structured information in portfolio decisions. Campos (2024) described credit-analysis and collection practices of microfinance institutions in Nueva Ecija, including the growing use of digital tools. Penaso and Dagang (2025) examined loan-management practices and operational efficiency in microfinance institutions in Northern Mindanao. Collectively, these studies support attention to organized records, loan monitoring, and collection workflows. FRAMS applies these ideas through borrower and loan records, installment schedules, payment tracking, and period reports; it does not claim to reproduce the portfolio-risk tool evaluated by Ramirez.

[140] Foreign Studies

[141] LendStack (2025) and BlueOrchard (2025) are cited in the original draft for discussion of cloud-based lending platforms and infrastructure costs. These sources are treated here as industry-oriented background rather than as equivalent to peer-reviewed empirical studies. FRAMS is designed for local hosting using Apache, PHP, and MySQL. Accordingly, the comparison concerns the use of web-based lending software, not a claim that FRAMS is cloud-hosted or that it achieves a particular cost reduction.

[142] Khandani et al. (2010) examined machine-learning models for consumer credit-risk assessment. Batista and Montero (2020) discussed a Brazilian digital micro-lending platform that combined automated scoring, borrower groups, and repayment-behavior updates. Chen et al. (2021) developed an early-warning approach using transaction and behavior data, while Juszczak et al. (2022) examined adaptive models that update risk assessments based on repayment behavior. Lopez and Martinez (2024) discussed modular architecture and continuous assessment in relation to system reliability. These studies demonstrate approaches that differ from a fixed, rule-based assessment. They inform the broader research context, but their reported outcomes should not be presented as outcomes achieved by FRAMS.

[143] The foreign studies also highlight the value of monitoring loans after release. In FRAMS, post-release monitoring is implemented through installment status, payment history, overdue-penalty records, and reports. These functions support staff review but do not constitute a machine-learning model, automated early-warning prediction, or continuous risk rescoring.

[144] Synthesis

[145] The local literature shows that microfinance lenders already practice credit checking, but much of it is manual, interview-based, and limited by the lack of a formal credit history (Mina, 2024; Juventud, Matigas, Himongala, & Galla, 2023; Juventud, Matigas, Himongala, Galla, & Abiol, 2023). Estrellado (2024) adds that access, repayment arrangements, and financial education matter to micro-business borrowers. Local studies then show the turn toward digital access and structured portfolio tools (Santos, 2023; Ramirez, 2024; Campos, 2024; Penaso & Dagang, 2025).

[146] The foreign literature and studies go further. They describe machine learning, alternative data, and continuous reassessment (Kou et al., 2022; Bazarbash & Beaton, 2020; Ghosh et al., 2023; Chen & Li, 2023; Frost et al., 2024; Rahman & Hasan, 2025; Khandani et al., 2010; Batista & Montero, 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Juszczak et al., 2022; Lopez & Martinez, 2024). Those capabilities are a research direction. They are not features of the system built in this study.

[147] The gap that FRAMS addresses is the absence of one web-based record that registers the borrower, stores the application and documents, applies a stated eligibility rule, releases the loan with a schedule, and tracks payments and penalties for a small lending office. FRAMS differs from the reviewed predictive systems because the eligibility result is explainable from income, debt, amount, term, and limits, and because the administrator remains the approving authority. [158] Conceptual Framework

[159] The conceptual framework of this study shows how FRAMS works. It explains how the system handles loan applications, loan release, and collection for RJ and RR Finance Services. The framework uses the Input–Process–Output (IPO) model, which shows how information flows from inputs, through the system, to outputs. The system takes information such as borrower registration and identity, employment, income, and obligations, the loan request and documents, and payment and penalty records from the administrator, collector, and borrower. FRAMS then uses this information to estimate eligibility, review documents, approve the loan, prepare the agreement, release the loan with a schedule, update payments, send notifications, and prepare reports. The outputs are the eligibility status for the reviewer, the released loan and installment schedule, the updated balance with its receipt and history, and period reports for the administrator. This helps the office make informed lending and collection decisions.

[160] Figure 1 Conceptual framework of the FRAMS

[161] Figure 1 presents the conceptual framework of the proposed FRAMS using the Input–Process–Output (IPO) model. The framework illustrates how data are collected as inputs, processed through the system, and transformed into meaningful outputs that support lending and collection. This framework is guided by literature on credit risk and micro-lending, which emphasizes the borrower’s identity, capacity to pay, supporting documents, and record of repayment.

[162] The Input component consists of the necessary data and users required for system operation. These include borrower registration and identity, such as the borrower’s personal details and account information. Employment, income, and obligations describe the borrower’s work, earnings, existing debts, and other monthly obligations used in the eligibility estimate. The loan request and documents state the amount, term, and purpose of the loan and supply the files needed for review. Payment and penalty records show amounts collected, remaining balances, and overdue charges. The administrator, collector, and borrower are also part of the input, because they encode, review, release, collect, and pay through the system.

[163] The Process component represents the operations performed by FRAMS. Approval covers the reviewer’s decision on the application. The eligibility estimate computes whether the requested loan fits the borrower’s income and existing obligations. Document review checks the files submitted with the application. The agreement records the approved terms accepted by the borrower. Loan release and schedule disburse the approved amount and set the installment plan. Payment update posts collections and penalty charges and refreshes the balance. Notifications inform the concerned users of application and payment events, and reports summarize lending and collection activity for a chosen period.

[164] The Output component consists of the processed information generated by the system. These outputs include the eligibility status for the reviewer, the released loan and installment schedule, the updated balance together with the receipt and payment history, and period reports for the administrator. These outputs provide organized and timely information for reviewing applications, releasing loans, monitoring balances, and checking collections.

[165] Overall, the conceptual framework demonstrates how FRAMS integrates input data, lending and collection processes, and informative outputs to support informed lending and collection decisions at RJ and RR Finance Services.

[166] Theoretical Framework

[167] The development of the Financial Risk Assessment and Micro-Lending System (FRAMS) is anchored on theories and models related to credit risk management, information system success, and technology acceptance. These foundations provide the basis for how FRAMS manages borrower information, evaluates loan requests, monitors repayment, and supports users in performing lending-related activities.

[168] Credit Risk and Loan Management. Credit risk management focuses on identifying and managing the possibility that a borrower may be unable to fulfill loan obligations. In microfinance, creditworthiness appraisal, loan monitoring, risk handling, and loan recovery are important components of effective loan management. Penaso and Dagang (2025) examined these areas in microfinance institutions in Northern Mindanao and found that loan management practices are associated with operational efficiency, particularly in payment and recovery activities.

[169] This concept is applied in FRAMS through the collection and evaluation of borrower information, income, existing obligations, requested loan amount, employment or business information, and supporting documents. The system provides an eligibility estimate based on defined lending criteria, such as the debt-to-income limit, minimum and maximum loan amount, and required employment or business duration. However, the system does not automatically reject applicants; the final decision remains with the authorized administrator. This allows FRAMS to function as a decision-support tool rather than replacing human judgment.

[170] DeLone and McLean Information Systems Success Model. DeLone and McLean (2003) proposed an updated Information Systems Success Model that evaluates information systems through dimensions including system quality, information quality, service quality, use, user satisfaction, and net benefits.

[171] The model is relevant to FRAMS because the system is designed to centralize loan and borrower information and make that information available for lending operations. In FRAMS, system quality is reflected in organized loan processing and monitoring functions, while information quality is supported by maintaining consistent borrower, loan, payment, and risk-assessment records. The resulting system is intended to improve the efficiency of loan processing, monitoring, reporting, and decision support.

[172] Technology Acceptance Model. Davis (1989) introduced the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which identifies perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as important factors influencing users' acceptance of information technology.

[173] This model is applicable to FRAMS because the system must be usable by the personnel responsible for processing and managing loans. FRAMS provides centralized borrower records, loan applications, payment monitoring, notifications, and reports to reduce the need for separate manual records. Its usefulness is reflected in its ability to support lending activities, while its ease of use is addressed through organized interfaces and role-based functions for administrators and other authorized users.

[174] These models also inform the User Acceptance Testing criteria used in Chapter IV. Functionality and reliability correspond to system quality in the DeLone and McLean model. Usability corresponds to perceived ease of use in the Technology Acceptance Model. Security corresponds to protected and role-restricted use. Performance corresponds to system quality as experienced during ordinary office use. The UAT ratings therefore represent user perceptions of these qualities. They are not independent laboratory measurements of response time or of residual security risk.
